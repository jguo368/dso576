Candidate Vision A: Provider Recruitment Prioritization

How might the decision be made today?

If I were a Medi-Cal workforce planning manager trying to decide which counties and provider specialties need more recruitment support, I would probably start by looking at the total number of providers in different counties and specialties.

I would then compare that information with the number of Medi-Cal members in each area. If one county has many Medi-Cal members but relatively few providers in a certain specialty, that might be a place worth looking at more closely.

I might also check other public data or reports to see whether the same area is already known to have a provider shortage. Before making a final decision, I would probably want to talk with people who know the local situation, such as health plans, county staff, or providers.


What information and people might be involved?

The manager might rely on information such as:

- Medi-Cal fee-for-service provider profile data
- Provider specialty and geographic location
- Whether providers are accepting new Medi-Cal patients
- Medi-Cal enrollment by county
- Demographic characteristics of Medi-Cal beneficiaries
- HCAI health workforce supply and shortage data
- Medi-Cal managed care network and enrollment information
- Complaints, access reports, or other evidence of difficulty obtaining care

People involved might include:

- DHCS workforce or data analysts
- Medi-Cal managed care plans
- HCAI workforce analysts
- County health departments
- Health care providers and provider organizations
- Community organizations serving Medi-Cal populations

What steps might they take?

A plausible process might include:

1. Review Medi-Cal provider counts by county and specialty.
2. Compare provider availability with Medi-Cal enrollment and population needs.
3. Identify counties and specialties that appear to have the largest gaps.
4. Compare these findings with broader California health workforce shortage data.
5. Review managed care or access information to determine whether the shortage is associated with actual difficulties accessing care.
6. Consult health plans, counties, or provider organizations to better understand local conditions.
7. Prioritize counties and specialties based on the severity of the provider gap, the number of Medi-Cal members affected, and the feasibility of provider recruitment.


Where might this process be difficult or uncertain?

The information needed for this decision may be distributed across several datasets and agencies. Different datasets may also use different geographic units, provider categories, and reporting periods.

Provider counts may not perfectly represent actual access to care. For example, a provider may appear in a directory but may not be accepting new patients or may have limited appointment availability.

As a result, the manager may need to manually combine multiple datasets and validate the findings with stakeholders before deciding where recruitment support is most needed.


Candidate Vision B: Access Support Prioritization

How might the decision be made today?

A Medi-Cal access strategy manager trying to prioritize language-access, telehealth, or transportation support might also need to combine several sources of information.

The manager could first identify counties and population groups that appear to experience barriers to care. They might then investigate whether those barriers are more closely associated with language needs, geographic distance, transportation limitations, or the ability to use telehealth.

For example, one population may have providers nearby but face language barriers, while another population may live far from available providers and benefit more from telehealth or transportation support.

What information and people might be involved?

The manager might rely on:

- Medi-Cal eligible counts and demographic data
- Preferred or written language information
- Provider language capabilities
- Medi-Cal managed care enrollment information
- Geographic distribution of providers
- Telehealth availability or utilization information
- Transportation programs and access information
- Network adequacy or timely-access measures
- Member complaints, surveys, or community feedback

People involved might include:

- DHCS access and policy staff
- Medi-Cal managed care plans
- County health agencies
- Community-based organizations
- Health care providers
- Transportation service providers
- Medi-Cal members and patient advocates

What steps might they take?

A plausible process might include:

1. Identify counties and populations that show signs of poor health care access.
2. Compare demographic information with provider availability.
3. Determine which barriers appear most important for each population.
4. Review whether existing language, telehealth, or transportation services already address those barriers.
5. Consult health plans, counties, and community organizations to validate the problem.
6. Match the main access barrier with a potential intervention.
7. Prioritize populations based on the severity of the access problem, the number of Medi-Cal members affected, equity considerations, and implementation feasibility.

Where might this process be difficult or uncertain?

Language access, provider capacity, transportation, telehealth, and demographic information may exist in separate datasets.

It may also be difficult to determine why a particular population has low access to care. For example, low telehealth utilization could result from limited broadband access, low digital literacy, patient preferences, or a lack of participating providers.

Different barriers may also overlap. A rural county could simultaneously experience provider shortages, long travel distances, and language-access challenges.

Therefore, a manager may need to combine quantitative data with local stakeholder knowledge before deciding which type of support should be prioritized.
