What can the core data honestly support?
The core data can support analysis of the geographic distribution and characteristics of Medi-Cal fee-for-service providers, such as where providers are located and what specialties are available. It can help identify areas with relatively limited provider presence. However, the core data alone cannot determine whether an area truly has insufficient access because it does not fully capture patient demand, managed-care enrollment, or broader workforce capacity.

Vision A
What does one row represent?
One row represents an enrolled Medi-Cal fee-for-service provider record associated with a provider/service location and provider specialty.

Which people, places, events, and time periods were actually observed?
The data observes enrolled Medi-Cal FFS providers and their service locations across California. It includes provider types and specialties, county and ZIP-code locations, and enrollment-status effective dates. It does not directly observe patients, health-care visits, or changes in provider availability over time.

Which exact columns could provide useful evidence?
NPI and Provider_Number can identify providers; FI_Provider_Type, FI_Provider_Specialty, ANC_Provider_Type, and Provider_Taxonomy describe provider types and specialties; and CountyName, FIPS_County_CD, ZIP, Latitude, and Longitude describe geographic distribution.

What important information was not observed?
The core data does not observe the number or health-care needs of Medi-Cal beneficiaries in each county, actual appointment availability, patient utilization, provider workload, or whether providers are accepting new patients. It also focuses on FFS providers and therefore does not fully capture managed-care provider capacity. Without a population or demand denominator, a low provider count alone cannot establish a provider shortage.

Could the evidence show an association, or support a causal claim?
The evidence can support descriptive comparisons and associations, such as identifying counties and specialties with relatively fewer enrolled FFS providers. However, it cannot support a causal claim that low provider counts cause poor access or that recruitment support would improve access, because patient demand and outcomes are not observed and no causal research design is present.




Vision B
What does one row represent?
One row represents an enrolled Medi-Cal FFS provider record associated with a provider/service location, rather than a Medi-Cal beneficiary or population group.

Which people, places, events, and time periods were actually observed?
The data observes enrolled Medi-Cal FFS providers and their service locations across California. It includes geographic information and some provider-level access characteristics, such as reported languages, telehealth services, and physical accessibility. It does not directly observe Medi-Cal beneficiaries, demographic groups, patient travel, or actual use of these services.

Which exact columns could provide useful evidence?
Useful columns include CountyName, FIPS_County_CD, ZIP, Latitude, and Longitude for geographic access; Threshold_Languages and Other_Languages for provider language capacity; Telehealth_Services for telehealth availability; and accessibility fields such as Acc_Exam_Room, Acc_Exterior_Building, Acc_Interior_Building, Acc_Parking, and Acc_Restroom for physical accessibility.

What important information was not observed?
The core data does not observe beneficiary demographics, preferred languages, disability status, internet access, transportation availability, travel time, or actual barriers experienced by patients. Therefore, it cannot directly identify which population groups are underserved or whether provider language and telehealth capacity matches local patient needs. Some access-related fields also appear to have substantial missing values, which would need to be assessed before using them.

Could the evidence show an association, or support a causal claim?
The data could support descriptive associations, such as whether some counties have fewer providers reporting certain language or telehealth capabilities. However, it cannot establish that these gaps cause poor access for particular population groups or that language, telehealth, or transportation interventions would improve access. Additional beneficiary-level and population data would be needed.
